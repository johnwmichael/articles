## Part One: Mason-Hamilton Road

My father grew up in rural Ohio, on Mason-Hamilton Road, outside the city of Hamilton. He was big enough and good enough to play college football as an offensive lineman — under Woody Hayes, one of the most storied coaches the game has known.

That's nearly all I have of that generation, and even less before it:

- My **grandmother was Austrian**. She spoke with an accent. Whatever else her life had been — a crossing of the Atlantic, a new language, a farm county in Ohio — I know her mainly as a sound and a presence from childhood.
- I **never met my grandfather**. He died young, and with him must have gone stories nobody thought to write down.

This is where the archive begins: not with a polished biography, but with what I actually have — a road in Ohio, a football coach's name, an accent, and an empty chair at the table.

## Part Two: The ICA — 4750 N. Sheridan Road

I didn't grow up on Mason-Hamilton Road. I grew up inside an organization: the **Institute of Cultural Affairs (ICA)**, headquartered at **4750 North Sheridan Road, Chicago** — an address I know the way other people know their childhood street.

What the ICA was (verified context, not memory):

- It grew out of the **Ecumenical Institute** in Chicago, and was incorporated separately in **1973** to carry its methods of human development to communities and organizations worldwide, on a secular footing.
- Its people included the **Order: Ecumenical** — a religious order formed primarily by *families of volunteers*, who lived and worked together in community.
- By the mid-1970s the ICA had expanded from Chicago to **over 100 houses in 30 countries** — a worldwide network of residential houses where colleagues lived communally and did community-development work.

What that meant for me is the next part of the story: a childhood lived inside a multinational community of families who had committed their lives to human development — and, eventually, streets in Kreuzberg.

## Part Three: Joining — Age Three, and a Camp in New Orleans

My parents joined the ICA when I was about three years old — roughly 1970–71.

As part of that joining, I was sent alone to a camp in New Orleans. I don't know its name. What I know is that I went, and that I went alone.

Research leads (archiveist notes, verified where marked):

- The OE/EI/ICA community maintains a repository at wiki.wedgeblade.net with firsthand accounts from second-generation members ("Children of the Order"). One account names "Camp New Jerusalem, 1971, in New Orleans" — which closely matches this timeline. *(Single source; unverified.)*
- The Ecumenical Institute had a religious house in New Orleans by 1971, consistent with a camp there. *(Verified from repository history documents.)*
- Hypothesis (to verify): new colleagues went through intensive training on joining, and children were housed separately during that period — a camp in New Orleans may have served that function.

## Part Four: The Map — A Childhood Across the ICA's World

The places I lived, in the order I remember them:

1. **Houston, Texas**
2. **Kansas City**
3. **Chicago**
4. **Majuro, Marshall Islands**
5. **West Berlin, Germany** — Kreuzberg
6. **Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia**
7. **Sungai Lui, Malaysia**
8. **A student house in Chicago**
9. **San Francisco** — through high school; we rented a flat on Laguna Street. I was three years old in Houston. We spent eight months in Majuro. Our family left the ICA about a year after arriving in San Francisco.

*(The exact Laguna Street address was shared as a Zillow link for family-history context; it is omitted from this public archive to avoid publishing a precise residential address.)*

What the historical record shows (verified, distinct from my memory):

- **Majuro:** The Marshall Islands Human Development Project was initiated in 1975 with a planning consult of 38 volunteer experts in business, community development, food production, education, and mechanics. (Shelterforce, 2017)
- **Sungai Lui:** An ICA Human Development Project in a Malay village, significant enough to be the subject of a peer-reviewed study — "A Malay Village That Progress Chose: Sungai Lui and the Institute of Cultural Affairs," *Human Organization*, Vol. 42, No. 1 (1983).
- **Kreuzberg Ost, Berlin:** Listed among the ICA's "Band of 24" Human Development Projects — a coordinated global program of community-development sites launched in the mid-to-late 1970s. (ICA Global Archives; Shelterforce, 2017)

**Archiveist observation:** Three of the places on my childhood list — Majuro, Kreuzberg Ost, Sungai Lui — appear on the ICA's documented project map. My childhood wasn't just *near* the ICA's work; it moved along the ICA's global network as it happened.

On the student house in Chicago: second-generation accounts describe a documented pattern where ICA children were gathered in Chicago around 6th–7th grade to live together in "student houses." The timeline fits my age. What it was like for me is mine to tell, and comes later in this record.

On my parents' joining: as I understand the family story, **my mother pushed the ICA decision and my father followed her into it.** That's my reading of what happened — family interpretation, not verified fact.

## Threads I Can See (archiveist observation, not settled fact)

- An Austrian grandmother may be the first German-language thread in the family — decades before I was the kid fluent in German in West Berlin.
- The ICA was, functionally, an international human network decades before "network intelligence" was a phrase — families moving between houses, shared methods, shared meals, shared purpose. It is hard to believe that left no mark on how I think about collaboration.
- How the Ohio football player's family ended up inside a Chicago-based human-development order is a story I don't yet have. That question goes near the top of the list.
